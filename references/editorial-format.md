# Editorial Format

Use this reference when writing or substantially revising an article.

## What the Format Should Achieve

The article should move the reader through this sequence:

**Answer → understand → compare or act → verify → decide**

The piece should feel edited, not assembled from a keyword list. Related search questions belong inside useful sections and FAQs, not as repetitive headings with near-identical answers.

## Opening

Answer the core question in the first one or two paragraphs. Then introduce the distinction that makes the rest of the guide necessary.

A strong opening usually does three things:

1. Defines the subject in plain language.
2. States the main benefit or answer.
3. Warns that products, methods, or outcomes differ in one material way.

Avoid scene-setting that delays the answer, generic claims that the topic is “revolutionizing” an industry, or a long list of what the article will cover.

## Headings and Sections

Use descriptive question-led or outcome-led headings. A reader should understand the purpose of a section from its heading alone.

Each major section should:

- Start with its direct answer.
- Explain the mechanism or reason.
- Add evidence, an example, or a table where useful.
- State a limitation when it changes the decision.

Combine overlapping questions. For example, “What is APY?” and “How is APY calculated?” can usually be one coherent section.

## Tables

Use a table when readers need to compare repeated fields across three or more items, or when a decision depends on several exact dimensions.

Good comparison fields include:

- Product or method
- Eligible asset or input
- How it works
- Published rate or result
- Fees
- Eligibility
- Custody or ownership
- Main risk or limitation

Write a short conclusion after the table. Do not leave readers to interpret it alone.

## Examples and Calculations

Use realistic, round-number examples. Show the formula, inputs, result, and what the result means.

For financial examples, include costs that could reverse the apparent benefit. If some costs are not modeled, name them directly beneath the calculation.

Do not disguise provider examples as independent evidence. Label them by source.

## Risks and Limitations

Place the most decision-relevant caveats in the main article, not only in a closing disclaimer.

Group risks by cause when helpful, such as:

- Asset or market risk
- Provider or custody risk
- Smart-contract or protocol risk
- Fee and liquidity risk
- Borrowing or liquidation risk
- Regulatory, tax, or geographic risk

Explain how each risk affects the reader. Avoid dumping legal language without interpretation.

## Ending

End with a practical selection checklist or next action. Summarize the decision rule rather than repeating the introduction.

Use internal links only where the linked page genuinely continues the reader's task. Avoid a wall of “related reading” links.

## Methodology Block

For a researched comparison, state:

- What types of sources were reviewed
- The verification date
- Whether products were tested or only documented
- How products were selected
- Whether the publisher has a commercial relationship with any product
- That live terms take precedence when public pages differ

## Production Notes

Put non-publishable instructions after a clear divider or in a separate companion section.

For every visual, use a table with:

| Placement | Asset | Required content | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|

Provide chart-ready data underneath. Include source labels, date labels, alt-text direction, and necessary disclaimers.
