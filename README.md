## The shared Semrush format

### 1. A clear, useful title

The title names the topic and the reader benefit. It does not use hype. Typical patterns are:

- What X is + how to do it
- X: complete guide + strategies
- Where/why/how X works + what to do next

### 2. Visible trust information before the article

Each article shows:

- Named author linked to a profile
- Named contributor or reviewer
- Publication or update date
- Estimated reading time
- Table of contents

The author profile states relevant experience and specialties. The contributor line shows editorial or subject-matter review.

### 3. A short, direct introduction

The introductions are normally two or three short paragraphs. They:

1. State the main point or problem immediately.
2. Explain why it matters.
3. Tell the reader what the article will cover.

There is no long story before answering the search intent.

### 4. Definition and context first

The first main section normally defines the topic in plain English. If confusion is likely, it immediately adds one of these:

- A comparison table
- A real-page example
- A screenshot
- A short list of types or components

### 5. Sections that answer their headings independently

Every H2 and H3 begins with a direct answer to its heading. A reader or an AI system can understand the section without needing the previous section.

A common section pattern is:

1. Direct answer or instruction
2. Short explanation of why it matters
3. Specific example, number, or scenario
4. Visual proof where useful
5. Practical action for the reader

### 6. Logical progression from understanding to action

The shared article flow is:

1. Define the subject.
2. Explain why it matters.
3. Break it into types, causes, or components.
4. Give a numbered method or framework.
5. Show real examples and evidence.
6. Explain mistakes, risks, or limitations.
7. End with the next practical step and a relevant CTA.

The exact mix changes with intent. A short explanatory article may use one main model and repeat “what it is” followed by “how to act.” A long tutorial may include both a build process and a troubleshooting section.

## The E-E-A-T system they use

### Experience

- First-person tests and observations are used when they genuinely prove a point.
- The writer shows real interfaces, searches, prompts, reports, or workflows.
- Realistic examples replace abstract claims.
- The enterprise article includes a personal migration example to contrast small-site and enterprise work.

For our next article, we should only claim first-hand experience when we have actually performed the test or have supplied evidence.

### Expertise

- The author biography states relevant years and areas of expertise.
- Technical terms are defined in simple language.
- Advice includes specific steps, thresholds, formulas, caveats, and decision criteria.
- Named contributors indicate subject-matter or editorial review.

For our next article, we should name the qualified author and reviewer, and identify which claims they reviewed.

### Authoritativeness

- Important claims link to primary or authoritative sources.
- Real brands and platforms are used as examples.
- Internal links connect the article to deeper topic-cluster resources.
- Proprietary product data or case studies are used when relevant and clearly identified.

For our next article, primary sources should support rules, product facts, technical behavior, numbers, and current claims. Internal links should be natural extensions of the reader's task.

### Trust

- Publication date, author, contributor, and profile are visible.
- Advice includes limitations and conditions rather than absolute promises.
- Quantitative claims include context and a source.
- Screenshots have descriptive captions or alt text.
- Product CTAs appear after useful instruction and are relevant to the section.

For our next article, every important number needs a source, date, scope, and clear definition. Promotional claims should not be disguised as independent evidence.

## Visual and data rules for the next article

Do not add decorative images just to break up text. Add a visual when it proves, explains, compares, or helps the reader complete a step.

### Use screenshots when

- Showing a product interface or setting
- Demonstrating a search result, AI response, or live example
- Giving step-by-step instructions
- Showing evidence for a result or claim

Each screenshot should have a purpose-led caption and descriptive alt text. Crop it to the relevant area and hide private information.

### Use diagrams when

- Explaining a flow, hierarchy, process, architecture, or cause-and-effect relationship
- The reader would struggle to picture the relationship from prose alone

Useful diagram types include a process flow, decision tree, hub-and-spoke model, before/after path, and layered system.

### Use tables when

- Comparing two or more options across the same criteria
- Summarizing roles, requirements, features, risks, or decisions
- Turning a framework into something the reader can use

Tables should add clarity, not repeat the surrounding paragraphs.

### Use charts when

- There is real quantitative data and a meaningful relationship to show
- The trend, comparison, distribution, or change is easier to understand visually

Every chart needs:

- Source
- Collection date or period
- Metric definition
- Sample or scope
- Labeled axes and units
- A one-sentence takeaway
- Any limitation that affects interpretation

Do not invent survey results or use unsupported numbers to make the article look authoritative.

### Use first-hand data when possible

Strong options include:

- A documented test with a stated method
- Product or analytics data with a defined date range
- A before/after comparison
- A small original study with its sample and limitations
- An expert quote that adds information not already in the draft

## Reusable outline for the next article

This is the default, not a rigid template. We will adjust it to the search intent.

1. **Title:** Topic + clear benefit or outcome
2. **Trust block:** Author, reviewer/contributor, date, reading time
3. **Introduction:** Main answer/problem, why it matters, what the guide covers
4. **H2: What is [topic]?** Direct definition
5. **H3: [Topic] vs. [commonly confused alternative]** Optional comparison table
6. **H2: Why [topic] matters** Benefits, risks, or consequences with evidence
7. **H2: How [topic] works** Types, components, or an explanatory model
8. **H2: How to [achieve outcome]** Numbered, actionable process
9. **H2: Examples, data, or case study** Real proof with method and source
10. **H2: Common mistakes or limitations** Problem, consequence, how to identify it, how to fix it
11. **H2: How to measure success** Relevant metrics, baseline, and review period
12. **H2: Next step** Brief conclusion and one relevant CTA

## Writing rules to preserve the feel

- Write for a knowledgeable beginner: plain language without dumbing down the subject.
- Use short paragraphs and descriptive headings.
- Lead each section with the answer.
- Prefer specific nouns, actions, and examples over vague marketing language.
- Define acronyms and technical terms on first use.
- Use numbered headings for processes and grouped lists.
- Add a “pro tip” only when it contains genuinely useful experience.
- Link descriptive anchor text, not “click here.”
- Use internal links where they help the reader continue a task.
- Use external links for evidence, especially primary sources.
- Separate facts, interpretation, and recommendations.
- State uncertainty and limitations where they exist.
- End with a concrete next step, not a generic summary.

## What not to copy

We should reproduce the editorial principles, not Semrush's wording or branding. The next article should use original language, original examples, relevant data, and the correct product or company voice. Its length and number of visuals should follow the topic's needs rather than imitate a fixed word count.
